What is real on this page, and what is not

Real

The surface. Every gyrus and every sulcus you see is measured, not sculpted: it is the fsaverage pial surface, built by FreeSurfer from the MRI of forty real human brains. 20,484 vertices, 40,960 triangles.

The regions. The colour shifts across the surface follow the Destrieux atlas — 75 named regions per hemisphere, assigned vertex by vertex.

The count and the density. 57,000 cell bodies in one cubic millimetre is the measured figure from H01 (Shapson-Coe et al., Science, 2024), a sample of human temporal cortex taken during epilepsy surgery, consented and anonymised. The way those cells thin out in layer I and crowd into layer IV is real cortical anatomy.

Not real

Where each cell sits. H01 publishes its cells as an image volume — 1.4 petabytes of electron microscopy — not as a table of coordinates. The 57,000 points in the small panel are placed at the correct density for their layer, not at the coordinates of those particular cells.

The exact spot. The sample came from the left temporal lobe. The cube is placed on the superior temporal gyrus using the atlas. It is the right neighbourhood, not the right address.

Any firing. Nothing here is simulated, and nothing is pretending to. To make neurons fire you need to know what is wired to what, and for human tissue nobody knows. That is the whole point of this page.

Why the fly can do what this cannot

A fruit fly has 138,639 neurons and every one of them has been traced, along with the 54,492,922 synapses between them. That is why a fly brain can be switched on and watched: the wiring exists. A human has about 620,000 times as many neurons, and the wiring diagram for them does not exist — not partially, not roughly. One cubic millimetre took a decade and a petabyte and a half.
